\begin{abstract}
Every Byzantine-robust aggregation rule in wide use---Krum, Multi-Krum,
Trimmed Mean, Median, FABA, Bulyan, Geometric Median (RFA),
Median-of-Means, and Divide-and-Conquer---shares one assumption: that a
single round's batch of client updates carries enough information to
separate malicious contributions from honest ones. We formalize two ways
this assumption fails. The \emph{Sybil/collusion blind spot} is an
identifiability limit: colluding attackers can make one round's batch
geometry statistically indistinguishable from a legitimately correlated
honest cohort, so no function of that batch alone can separate them. The
\emph{non-IID conflation problem} is its dual: any per-client trust
signal monotonic in ``distance from what looks typical this round''
carries information about \emph{how much} a client deviates but none
about \emph{why}, so a legitimately unusual honest client is scored
exactly as an attacker producing the same deviation.

We measure both directly against faithful implementations of twelve
published methods, across ten sweep experiments, two diagnostics-driven
investigations, and a real-data validation---\textbf{28{,}331 result
rows}, five seeds per synthetic cell with 95\% confidence intervals. The
baseline characterization yields three findings not previously documented
as distinguishing properties \emph{within} the robust family: a
survivor-set-size failure mode that splits the family into methods that
collapse by ${>}1000\times$ when the assumed Byzantine fraction is
exceeded and methods that degrade gracefully (\S\ref{sec:paramismatch});
a structural split in which methods pay a real non-IID fairness cost and
which do not, predicted by whether the combining step is a single
central-tendency statistic (\S\ref{sec:fairness}); and a $5.6\times$
degradation of FoolsGold against colluders that share an objective but
not a byte stream---a limitation of a published method under a threat
model its own paper does not test (\S\ref{sec:correlated}).

We then propose, build, ablate, and stress-test \textbf{Deviation
Stability Scoring (DSS)}, a cross-round wrapper that scores clients by
the temporal variance of their deviation trace and the pairwise
correlation between traces. We report it as measured rather than as
hoped. DSS works as designed against temporally unstable colluders
($161.3 \rightarrow 1.18$ mean distance-from-truth wrapping FedAvg);
provides no protection against stable ones, by construction; protects a
genuinely non-IID honest minority only \emph{asymptotically}, with a
measured 6--13 round transient window in which that minority is wrongly
suppressed alongside the real attackers; and---until repaired here---could
make an already-robust base method up to $577\times$ \emph{worse} than
leaving it alone. Our ablation shows the conjunction rule at DSS's core
discards real signal: against non-identical stable colluders a
collusion-only gate scores $1.09$ where the shipped conjunction scores
$17.13$, no better than no defense. We also report a numerical defect
(catastrophic cancellation in \code{f32}) whose repair made results
reproducible but \emph{not} better, disconfirming our own published
diagnosis of the failure it was blamed for. A first real-data check on
MNIST reproduces the central robustness result on a 50{,}890-parameter
model, and finds that Centered Clipping at its commonly-shipped default
radius scores \emph{worse than no defense at all}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Federated learning trains a shared model across many clients' private
data without centralizing it~\cite{mcmahan2017}. Because the server never
observes client data, it cannot verify that a submitted update is honest:
a client, or a coalition, may submit an arbitrary malicious update
instead. This is the \emph{Byzantine-robust aggregation} problem---design
an aggregation rule $A$ that still converges despite up to $f$ of $n$
clients behaving arbitrarily~\cite{blanchard2017}.

A large literature has produced many such rules, each defending with a
different geometric or statistical signal (\S\ref{sec:related}). This
paper asks a question that cuts across all of them: \textbf{do these
methods share a structural limitation independent of which specific
signal each one uses?} We argue yes. Every one of them computes its
decision at round $t$ as a function of round $t$'s batch alone, and that
single-round-only evaluation is itself the limiting factor---not any
individual method's scoring rule.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Two formal claims} (\S\ref{sec:problem}) isolating what
    single-round aggregation cannot do: a Sybil/collusion
    \emph{identifiability} limit, and a non-IID \emph{conflation} limit.
    Both are stated as properties of the function signature
    $A^{(t)} = f(\{u_i^{(t)}\})$, not of any particular $f$.

  \item \textbf{A measured characterization of twelve published methods}
    (\S\ref{sec:baseline}) under six attacks, yielding three findings we
    have not seen documented as distinguishing properties within the
    robust family: the survivor-set-size failure mode
    (\S\ref{sec:paramismatch}), the fairness-cost structural split
    (\S\ref{sec:fairness}), and FoolsGold's degradation against
    non-identical colluders (\S\ref{sec:correlated}).

  \item \textbf{Deviation Stability Scoring} (\S\ref{sec:dss}), a
    cross-round wrapper composing with any existing rule, together with
    an honest account of what it does and does not achieve
    (\S\ref{sec:dssresults}).

  \item \textbf{An ablation} (\S\ref{sec:ablation}) that isolates which of
    DSS's two signals does the work, and shows the conjunction rule
    combining them has a real, quantified cost.

  \item \textbf{A first real-data validation} (\S\ref{sec:realdata})
    establishing which synthetic conclusions transfer to a real model and
    which do not---including a negative result about a shipped default
    that a deployment could reasonably mistake for a recommendation.

  \item \textbf{Three conclusions we overturned by measurement}
    (\S\ref{sec:overturned}), reported as such. Two were our own prior
    published diagnoses; one was a claim about a mechanism that turned out
    to be an artifact of the attack model used to test it.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{A note on how results are reported}

This paper reports several results that are unfavorable to its own
proposal, and one section (\S\ref{sec:overturned}) devoted entirely to
conclusions that measurement overturned. This is deliberate. The single
most useful finding in \S\ref{sec:dssresults}---that a wrapper can make
the method it wraps dramatically worse---was invisible to design
reasoning and appeared only on contact with data. We report the negative
space accordingly.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Distance- and selection-based defenses.}
Krum and Multi-Krum~\cite{blanchard2017} score each update by its summed
squared distance to its nearest neighbors, keeping the lowest-scoring one
(Krum) or the lowest-scoring $n-f$ (Multi-Krum). FABA~\cite{xia2019}
iteratively removes whichever update is farthest from the running mean of
what remains. Bulyan~\cite{elmhamdi2018} re-runs Krum-style scoring on a
shrinking pool, then combines survivors with a coordinate-wise trimmed
mean.

\paragraph{Coordinate-wise and whole-vector statistics.}
Trimmed Mean and Median~\cite{yin2018} combine each coordinate
independently. Median-of-Means~\cite{chen2017} partitions the batch into
groups, averages within each, and takes the median of the group means.
Geometric Median / RFA~\cite{pillutla2019} instead combines the whole
vector jointly via Weiszfeld's algorithm, preserving cross-coordinate
structure the per-coordinate methods discard.

\paragraph{Spectral defenses.}
Divide-and-Conquer~\cite{shejwalkar2021} scores each update by its
squared projection onto the batch's top principal component, removing
those contributing most to the dominant variance direction---designed
explicitly against optimized attacks that defeat distance-based methods.

\paragraph{History-aware defenses.}
FoolsGold~\cite{fung2020} is, to our knowledge, the only widely-cited
method in this landscape that uses cross-round information: it accumulates
each client's historical update and down-weights clients whose
\emph{history} is suspiciously similar to another's---the signature of
Sybils reinforcing each other over time. Centered
Clipping~\cite{karimireddy2021} also carries state across rounds, but uses
it differently: it maintains one persistent server-held reference vector
and clips every update to radius $\tau$ around it, bounding influence
rather than scoring clients against each other. These two are the closest
prior art to this paper's proposal, and we compare against both directly.

The closest prior art of all, however, is
FLANDERS~\cite{gabrielli2024}, which we compare against in
\S\ref{sec:flanders}. FLANDERS treats the sequence of local models as a
matrix-valued time series $\Theta_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times h}$
($d$ parameters $\times$ $h$ clients), fits a first-order matrix
autoregressive model $\Theta_t = A\Theta_{t-1}B + E_t$ by alternating
least squares, and retains the top-$k$ clients whose observed updates
lie closest to the forecast. Like DSS it is a \emph{pre-aggregation
filter} composing over an arbitrary base rule, and like DSS it derives
its signal from cross-round history rather than from within-round
geometry. It targets explicitly the regime where malicious clients
outnumber legitimate ones. We therefore do not claim the
filter-over-arbitrary-base shape as a contribution: FLANDERS has it.
What separates the two is the \emph{dimensionality of the signal} --- a
$d \times h$ matrix forecast against a length-$w$ scalar trace --- and,
as \S\ref{sec:flanders} measures, the fact that the two methods fail on
opposite attack shapes.

\paragraph{Trusted-reference defenses.}
FLTrust~\cite{cao2021} and Zeno~\cite{xie2019} score updates against a
reference the server computes itself---from a small trusted root dataset,
or a validation-loss check---rather than anything derived from the client
batch. This makes them structurally immune to the collusion argument in
\S\ref{sec:claim1}, at the cost of requiring the server to train on real
data.

\paragraph{Optimized and adaptive attacks.}
Fang et al.~\cite{fang2020} show Krum, Multi-Krum, Trimmed Mean, and
Median can each be defeated by an attack optimized against that defense's
own decision boundary. Baruch et al.~\cite{baruch2019} (``A Little Is
Enough'', ALIE) shift each coordinate by a calibrated multiple of the
honest population's own standard deviation, small enough to stay
statistically plausible. Li et al.~\cite{li2019} negate and scale the
honest consensus direction. Bagdasaryan et al.~\cite{bagdasaryan2020}
boost a chosen malicious direction so it dominates a plain average. None
of these five is \emph{temporally} consistent---each is optimal for its
round alone, which is precisely the property \S\ref{sec:attacks}'s two new
attacks exist to supply.

\paragraph{The robustness--fairness tension.}
That Byzantine-robust methods can systematically disadvantage
legitimately non-IID clients is discussed as a stated limitation across
the post-2020 robustness literature. We are not aware of a method that
explicitly attempts to disentangle ``malicious'' from ``legitimately
unusual'' using temporal information, which is the gap
\S\ref{sec:dss} targets.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Setting}

A standard federated round has $n$ clients, up to $f$ of them Byzantine.
Client $i$ holds data drawn from distribution $\mathcal{D}_i$, not
necessarily identical across clients (the non-IID case). At round $t$,
client $i$ submits update $u_i^{(t)} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and an
aggregation rule computes
\[
  \theta^{(t+1)} \;=\; A\!\left(u_1^{(t)}, \dots, u_n^{(t)}\right).
\]

Every method surveyed in \S\ref{sec:related}, with the two noted
exceptions, computes $A$ as a function of round $t$'s batch \textbf{alone}:
\begin{equation}
  A^{(t)} \;=\; f\!\left(\{u_i^{(t)}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)
  \label{eq:singleround}
\end{equation}
via one of three signals: pairwise distance (Krum, Multi-Krum, FABA,
Bulyan's selection step), coordinate rank or grouped mean (Trimmed Mean,
Median, Median-of-Means), or batch variance structure (Geometric Median,
Divide-and-Conquer). None conditions on $t-1, t-2, \dots$.

\subsection{Claim 1 --- the Sybil/collusion blind spot}
\label{sec:claim1}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Claim.} For any $A$ whose decision at round $t$ is a function of
$\{u_i^{(t)}\}$ alone, there exist two data-generating processes at round
$t$---(a) $n$ honest clients where a genuine subset $S \subset [n]$ share
correlated data (clients from the same organization or region), and (b)
$n - |S|$ honest clients plus $|S|$ colluding Sybils whose updates are
crafted to be mutually similar---that induce the same distribution over
$\{u_i^{(t)}\}$, up to a choice of crafted values matching the honest
sub-cohort's own statistics. Since $A$'s decision cannot depend on
anything outside $\{u_i^{(t)}\}$, it cannot distinguish (a) from (b).
\end{quote}

This is an \emph{identifiability} limit, not a weakness of any particular
choice of $f$. No amount of improvement to a single-round scoring rule
can resolve it, because the information required is absent from the rule's
input.

\subsection{Claim 2 --- the non-IID conflation problem}
\label{sec:claim2}

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Claim.} For any $A$ whose per-client trust signal is monotonic in
``distance from what looks typical this round''---true of all the
single-round methods above, since that is their entire mechanism---a
client $i$ whose $\mathcal{D}_i$ is a genuine minority within the
participant population is scored identically to an attacker producing the
same geometric deviation. Formally, if
$s_i^{(t)} = g\!\left(\lVert u_i^{(t)} - r^{(t)} \rVert\right)$ for some
reference $r^{(t)}$ and monotone decreasing $g$, then $s_i$ carries no
information about \emph{why} $u_i$ deviates---only \emph{how much}.
\end{quote}

This predicts a measurable fairness cost: client $i$'s expected influence
becomes a decreasing function of $\mathcal{D}_i$'s divergence from the
population mode, independent of whether $i$ is honest.
\S\ref{sec:fairness} confirms it, with a sharper structure than the
claim's general form predicts.

\subsection{Why both claims matter together}

Both are consequences of the same root cause: Eq.~\ref{eq:singleround}
depends only on round $t$. A method with access to
$\{u_i^{(t-k)}\}_{k>0}$ has, in principle, information neither claim's
argument rules out:

\begin{itemize}
  \item A genuinely non-IID honest client's deviation should be
    \textbf{stable} round over round---same local data, same
    characteristic skew.
  \item A colluding Sybil cluster's deviation is, by construction,
    \textbf{correlated with other specific clients}.
  \item An attacker adapting round to round against a shifting decision
    boundary should show \textbf{instability}.
\end{itemize}

\S\ref{sec:dss} turns this observation into a mechanism.

\section{Proposed Method: Deviation Stability Scoring}
\label{sec:dss}

\subsection{Hypothesis}

A legitimately non-IID honest client's deviation signal $d_i^{(t)}$
should be temporally \emph{stable}: the same local distribution produces a
similar characteristic deviation round after round. An attacker adaptively
evading a shifting decision boundary should produce a temporally
\emph{unstable} signal instead. Sybils, additionally, should show
deviation traces \emph{correlated with each other}.

\subsection{Mechanism}
\label{sec:mechanism}

DSS is a wrapper around any existing single-round rule $A$, not an
additional standalone aggregator.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Deviation extraction.} Take $A$'s per-client per-round
    deviation signal $d_i^{(t)}$. For methods that compute one internally
    (Krum, FABA, Bulyan, DnC) it is read off directly; otherwise
    $d_i^{(t)} = \lVert u_i^{(t)} - \theta^{(t+1)} \rVert$.

  \item \textbf{Rolling trace.} Maintain a per-client window of length
    $w$: $\mathrm{trace}_i = [d_i^{(t-w)}, \dots, d_i^{(t)}]$. We use
    $w = 5$.

  \item \textbf{Stability score.}
    \begin{equation}
      S_i \;=\; \frac{1}{1 + \mathrm{Var}(\mathrm{trace}_i)}
      \;\in\; (0, 1]
    \end{equation}
    High for a consistent trace, low for an erratic one.

  \item \textbf{Collusion score.} Pairwise cosine similarity of clients'
    deviation \emph{traces}---not raw updates:
    \begin{equation}
      C_i \;=\; \max_{j \neq i}
        \frac{\langle \mathrm{trace}_i, \mathrm{trace}_j \rangle}
             {\lVert \mathrm{trace}_i \rVert \, \lVert \mathrm{trace}_j \rVert}
    \end{equation}
    This generalizes FoolsGold's history-similarity idea to operate on the
    deviation signal rather than the raw gradient history, so it composes
    with any base method's own notion of ``unusual''. The trace has
    dimension $w \leq 5$ rather than the model size, making the signal
    model-size-independent---a trade whose real cost we quantify in
    \S\ref{sec:numerical}.

  \item \textbf{The conjunction gate.} A client is distrusted iff
    \begin{equation}
      S_i < \tau_S \;\;\wedge\;\; C_i > \tau_C,
      \qquad \tau_S = 0.5,\; \tau_C = 0.8.
      \label{eq:gate}
    \end{equation}
    The conjunction is the design's load-bearing choice. A client with low
    stability but low collusion---the legitimately-noisy, independent
    honest client of Claim 2---is left at $A$'s baseline weight. Punishing
    instability alone would reintroduce Claim 2's problem in a new form.

  \item \textbf{Combine through the base method.} DSS drops
    fully-distrusted clients, scales survivors' sample counts by their
    weight, and calls $A$ on the re-weighted batch. Krum still selects;
    Trimmed Mean still trims. A non-firing gate degrades to \emph{the base
    method}, which is the floor a wrapper must always have.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Step 6 is a repair, not an original design choice.}
The first implementation used $A$'s output only to compute the deviation
reference, then combined the raw batch itself with DSS's weights. That is
the defect \S\ref{sec:finding3} measures and fixes; we state the corrected
mechanism here and report the original's failure honestly rather than
presenting the repair as foresight. \emph{Dropping} matters as much as
scaling: selection-based methods ignore sample counts entirely, so a
client scaled to zero would still be a candidate they could select.
Removing it is the only way DSS's judgment reaches those methods at all.

Three things make the combination a genuine, if narrow, contribution

signal, which distinguishes DSS from both FoolsGold's
full-dimensionality gradient histories and FLANDERS's $d \times h$

from nothing. We do \emph{not} claim DSS is a large algorithmic advance.
Each ingredient has real precedent. What is new is the specific synthesis,
evaluated against this paper's own Claim 1 / Claim 2 formalization---and
that evaluation surfaced two implementation-level regressions, one
numerical defect, and one previously unstated transient failure window
that a claim resting on the hypothesis alone would have missed.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Implementations under test}

All twelve aggregation rules are faithful, citation-checked
implementations in a common Rust framework, exercised through one
\code{Aggregator} trait so that no method receives special handling. Every
method is the published one: where a framework-imposed behavior would
deviate from a paper's definition, it is opt-in and off by default.
FoolsGold in particular was verified line-by-line against the authors'
own reference implementation after an initial from-prose draft proved to
have a real bug.

\subsection{Synthetic protocol}

Unless a table states otherwise: ground truth $\theta^{*} = (1,\dots,1)$,
$d = 3$; honest clients drawn from $\mathcal{N}(\theta^{*}, 0.3^2 I)$; 8
honest clients and 2 attackers; 20 rounds; \textbf{5 seeds per cell} with
95\% confidence intervals throughout. The fairness experiment
(\S\ref{sec:fairness}) uses 20 seeds, its leave-one-out signal being
sparser.

The low dimensionality is deliberate: it isolates the aggregation rule
from every optimization confound. It is also the setup's principal
external-validity risk, and \S\ref{sec:realdata} is where we test whether
the conclusions survive contact with a real model. One does not.

\subsection{Metrics}

\begin{description}
  \item[Distance from truth.] $\lVert \hat\theta - \theta^{*}\rVert_2$.
    The primary metric---always well defined. \emph{Lower is better.}

  \item[Attack Success Rate.]
    $\lVert \hat\theta - \theta^{*}\rVert / \lVert \bar u_{\text{atk}} -
    \theta^{*}\rVert$, where $\bar u_{\text{atk}}$ is the mean of what
    attackers actually submitted: how much of the attacker's own reach
    survived, relative to how far it was reaching. $0$ = fully defended,
    $1$ = attack fully succeeded. Undefined with no attackers.

  \item[Non-IID Fairness Gap.] Realized as \emph{leave-one-out influence},
    $\lVert A(\text{batch}) - A(\text{batch} \setminus \{i\})\rVert$: how
    much removing client $i$ actually changes the aggregate. Chosen over
    an inclusion-rate metric because it treats every method as a black
    box, which selection-set-based metrics cannot.

    \textbf{A correction found while implementing it}: raw leave-one-out
    influence is confounded by a point's own extremity---an unweighted
    mean naturally moves more when \emph{any} extreme point is removed,
    fair treatment or not. The real metric normalizes against
    \emph{FedAvg's own leave-one-out influence on the same batch and
    seed}, FedAvg applying no filtering and thus serving as the
    ``no discrimination'' reference. NFG is then the slope of minority
    clients' \emph{relative} influence as divergence grows: $0$ or
    positive = no fairness cost; negative = the method discounts
    legitimate diversity relative to an undiscriminating baseline.
\end{description}

\subsection{Attacks}
\label{sec:attacks}

Four are published; two are introduced here to supply the temporal
consistency the published ones lack.

\begin{description}
  \item[Gaussian~\cite{blanchard2017}.]
    $u_{\text{atk}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$, independent per attacker.
  \item[Sign-flipping~\cite{li2019}.]
    $u_{\text{atk}} = -c \cdot \mathrm{mean}(\{u_{\text{honest}}\})$.
  \item[ALIE~\cite{baruch2019}.]
    $u_{\text{atk}} = \mathrm{mean} - z \cdot \mathrm{std}$
    coordinate-wise, with
    $z = \Phi^{-1}\!\left(\frac{n-m-\lfloor n/2+1\rfloor+m}{n-m}\right)$:
    the largest per-coordinate shift a majority argument still cannot
    distinguish from the honest population.
  \item[Scaling~\cite{bagdasaryan2020}.]
    $u_{\text{atk}} = \mathrm{mean} + s\,(\text{target} - \mathrm{mean})$.
  \item[Persistent Sybil \emph{(this work)}.]
    $u_{\text{atk}} = c$, a fixed constant, identical every round,
    independent of the honest batch. \emph{Temporally stable by
    construction}---the adversarial case for a stability-based detector.
  \item[Adaptive Evasion \emph{(this work)}.]
    $u_{\text{atk}}^{(t)} = m^{(t)} \hat d$ along a fixed direction, with
    \[
      m^{(t)} = m^{(t-1)} \cdot
      \begin{cases}
        \kappa_{\uparrow} & \text{if } \mathrm{actual} \leq \mathrm{expected} + \text{margin}\\
        \kappa_{\downarrow} & \text{otherwise,}
      \end{cases}
    \]
    where $\mathrm{actual}$ measures how far last round's aggregate landed
    from the submission, and $\mathrm{expected}$ is the same quantity for a
    hypothetical undefended sample-weighted average---i.e.\ what pure
    minority-share dilution alone would produce. Only $\mathrm{actual}$
    exceeding $\mathrm{expected}$ counts as ``a real defense is active'';
    escalate otherwise. $\kappa_{\uparrow}=1.2$,
    $\kappa_{\downarrow}=0.5$, margin $=0.15$.

    This is explicitly \emph{not} a reproduction of Fang et
    al.~\cite{fang2020}'s optimization-based search---it is a deliberately
    transparent local hill-climbing heuristic, chosen so its behavior is
    exactly verifiable. Its verification: unbounded escalation against
    undefended FedAvg ($20\times$ higher final distance than a
    fixed-magnitude attack), versus near-identical steady-state magnitude
    to the fixed attack against every real defense---confirming escalation
    continues only when nothing resists it.

  \item[Correlated Sybil \emph{(this work, \S\ref{sec:correlated})}.]
    Colluders pull toward a shared objective but each adds its own offset
    drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0, \text{divergence}^2)$: correlated yet
    individually distinguishable, and with fixed offsets, \emph{temporally
    stable}. At divergence $=0$ it reproduces Persistent Sybil exactly
    (asserted by a unit test), making it a strict generalization rather
    than a separate attack.
\end{description}

\subsection{Finding: a survivor-set-size failure mode, not a universal one}
\label{sec:paramismatch}

Figure~\ref{fig:exp21}'s aggregate view makes the Scaling attack look
uniformly dangerous. Breaking it down by attacker count reveals a sharper
story.

At 1--2 attackers (11--20\%, at or under the assumed $f$) every defended
method holds. At 3 attackers (27\%, exceeding the assumed 20\%),
Multi-Krum, FABA, and Divide-and-Conquer \textbf{collapse to within noise
of undefended FedAvg}---a jump from ${\sim}0.09$ to ${\sim}95$, a
${>}1000\times$ degradation in a single step. Krum, Median,
Median-of-Means, and Geometric Median degrade far more gracefully across
the same transition.

\paragraph{Mechanism.} This is a \emph{survivor-set-size} effect, not a
scoring-quality effect. Multi-Krum keeps exactly
$n - \lfloor \text{byzantine\_fraction} \cdot n \rfloor$ updates; when the
true attacker count exceeds the assumed $f$, attacker updates are
\emph{guaranteed} to survive into the trusted set regardless of how well
the scoring ranks them. Trimmed Mean and FABA share the same structural
exposure---both trim a \emph{count} derived from the assumed fraction, not
from what the batch actually contains. Krum (always keeps exactly one),
Median and Median-of-Means (no hard survivor-set size), and Geometric
Median (continuous reweighting, no discrete cutoff) are structurally
insulated.

\paragraph{Confirmed by a matched re-run.} Re-running with the Byzantine
fraction matched to the true attacker fraction at each step resolves the
collapse completely: Bulyan $0.053 \rightarrow 0.119$, Multi-Krum and FABA
flat at $0.054$--$0.091$, Trimmed Mean $0.169 \rightarrow 0.358$, DnC
$0.091$--$0.135$ across the whole sweep. The Scaling attack has
\textbf{no residual advantage} against any of these methods once correctly
parameterized. \S\ref{sec:paramismatch}'s collapse was entirely an
artifact of an under-specified parameter---not a property of the attack,
and not of the methods' scoring quality.

The general principle---that Byzantine-robust guarantees require the
assumed bound to hold---is implicit in each cited paper's own theorem
statement. What we have not seen documented is the \emph{differential}
exposure across methods sharing the same nominal threat model, which is a
direct product of this experiment and a practical deployment concern: a
deployment that mis-estimates $f$ does not degrade uniformly across its
options.

\subsection{Finding: the non-IID fairness cost splits structurally}
\label{sec:fairness}

11 aggregators $\times$ 6 minority-shift values $\{0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3\}$
$\times$ 20 seeds, \textbf{zero attackers}, 6 majority + 2 minority honest
clients. For equal-covariance Gaussians the KL divergence between minority
and majority is proportional to the squared shift, making shift a
principled divergence axis rather than an arbitrary knob.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{\detokenize{figure2_noniid_conflation.png}}
  \caption{Experiment 2.3, 10{,}560 rows. \emph{Real measurement, not
  illustrative.} Confirms Claim 2---with a sharper finding than the claim's
  general form predicts.}
  \label{fig:fairness}
\end{figure}

Two clean groups emerge, split along a structural line rather than
uniformly across ``the robust family'' as \S\ref{sec:claim2}'s argument
alone would suggest:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Strong fairness cost}---Krum, Median, Bulyan, Geometric
    Median. At shift $3.0$, minority influence falls to \textbf{22--39\%}
    of the FedAvg-normalized baseline. All four \emph{started above}
    $1.0$: a genuinely non-IID minority is more influential than average
    at low divergence, and that advantage then inverts and collapses as
    divergence grows.

  \item \textbf{Weak or no fairness cost}---Multi-Krum, FABA,
    Divide-and-Conquer. Relative influence stays essentially flat
    (1.28--1.44) across the entire range. Trimmed Mean is the most
    striking: relative influence \emph{increases} with shift
    ($1.13 \rightarrow 1.44$), the opposite of Claim 2's predicted
    direction.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Mechanism, consistent with \S\ref{sec:paramismatch}.} The
methods showing strong fairness cost are exactly those whose combining
step is \emph{a single central-tendency statistic that discounts distance
from itself}---Krum's nearest-neighbor score, Median's and Geometric
Median's robust-center definitions, Bulyan's Krum-based selection.
Discounting distance from center is definitionally what these methods do
for robustness, so a legitimately distant honest minority pays exactly the
price an attacker would. Multi-Krum, FABA, and DnC keep a \emph{wider}
survivor set ($n-f$ rather than a single point), giving divergent-but-honest
clients room to survive.

Trimmed Mean's reversal is genuinely surprising and \emph{not} explained
by this account. We flag it as an open question rather than absorb it into
the narrative.

FoolsGold sits outside both groups: highest of all eleven at shift $0$
($2.15\times$) and still above the FedAvg baseline at shift $3.0$
($1.42\times$)---consistent with it not filtering by geometric distance
from a center at all. This experiment is single-round, so its cross-round
mechanism is barely exercised here.

\section{Results: DSS Evaluation}

\subsection{The confirming result}
\label{sec:dsswin}

Against a temporally unstable colluding pair, wrapping FedAvg in DSS

\[
  \text{\code{fedavg}: } 161.345
  \;\longrightarrow\;
  \text{\code{dss\_fedavg}: } \mathbf{1.178}
  \qquad (\text{Adaptive Evasion, } {>}130\times)
\]
This is the hypothesis of \S\ref{sec:dss} working, on the attack shape it
targets. It is the paper's one unambiguous positive result about DSS, and
we state its scope precisely in the next subsection rather than let it
stand for more than it is.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{\detokenize{figure5_experiment_2_4_dss_validation.png}}
  \caption{Experiment 2.4, DSS validation, 1{,}400 rows.}
  \label{fig:dssval}

\subsection{The scope boundary: stable colluders}

DSS does \textbf{not} catch temporally stable colluders. Against
Persistent Sybil, \code{dss\_fedavg} scores $16.99$---exactly undefended
FedAvg's number. This is not a caveat that might not matter in practice;
it is a structural consequence of Eq.~\ref{eq:gate}. Low deviation
variance is precisely what makes a stable attacker stable, so the
stability half of the conjunction never fires and the gate never closes.
Anyone deploying DSS should not expect it to help against a Sybil pool
that simply repeats the same contribution every round. FoolsGold catches
exactly this case ($1.345$), for a different reason: it does not gate on
instability at all.

\subsection{Finding 3: a wrapper that made its base method worse}
\label{sec:finding3}

This is the most serious problem we found in our own proposal, and it was
invisible to design reasoning.

\paragraph{The defect.} DSS originally used the base method's output only
to compute a deviation reference, then combined the raw batch itself.
Stable colluders never trip the conjunction, so every weight stayed at
$1.0$---and a weighted mean with uniform weights \emph{is} FedAvg.
Wrapping Krum in DSS therefore silently replaced Krum with FedAvg whenever
DSS had no opinion, discarding Krum's exclusion along with it. Measured:
\code{dss\_krum} at $16.99$ against Persistent Sybil where plain
\code{krum} measured $0.297$---a $57\times$ regression \emph{caused by the
wrapper}. Against the Scaling attack the same mechanism produced a
$577\times$ regression ($171.47$ vs.\ $0.297$).

The defect was confirmed independently in the solo-attacker setting
(\S\ref{sec:solo}) before it was understood---the same mechanism, two
structurally different scenarios.

\paragraph{The repair and its measurement.} Step 6 of
\S\ref{sec:mechanism} is the fix: apply DSS's judgment \emph{through} the
base method. Experiment 2.8 (3{,}600 rows) measures the bare base, the
original combine (\code{dssraw\_}), and the repair (\code{dss\_}) side by
side under the same design.

\subsection{The joint case: asymptotically correct, transiently wrong}
\label{sec:joint}

\S\ref{sec:fairness} tested fairness with zero attackers;
\S\ref{sec:dsswin}--\S\ref{sec:solo} tested attack resistance with clean
IID honest batches. Neither alone answers the question both claims exist
to raise together: \textbf{does DSS protect a genuinely non-IID honest
minority at the same time it suppresses real colluders?}

We built a joint scenario: 6 majority honest clients, 2 minority honest
clients at mean $1.0 + 3.0$ with \emph{independently resampled noise every
round} (genuine round-to-round variance, unlike \S\ref{sec:fairness}'s
fixed shift), and 2 colluding Adaptive Evasion attackers, all in the same
batch. \code{dss\_fedavg}, 20 rounds, 5 seeds.

\paragraph{The asymptotic claim holds.} In \emph{every} seed, the steady
state by round 20 is exactly what \S\ref{sec:dss}'s hypothesis predicts:
the non-IID minority recovers to weight $1.0$---fully protected---while
both attackers converge to weight $0.0$, fully suppressed. DSS does
eventually achieve the joint outcome both claims call for, simultaneously.

configuration. That is not a guarantee for any other.

\subsection{A numerical defect, and what fixing it did not do}
\label{sec:numerical}

Per-client instrumentation traced the solo-attacker failure
(\S\ref{sec:solo}) to a concrete arithmetic defect: the final weight
$1 - C_i$ was computed in \code{f32}. Near $1.0$, \code{f32}'s ULP is
$1.19 \times 10^{-7}$, so $1.0 - 0.999998$ retains barely one significant
digit---and that digit is rounding error. Measured collusion among nine
\emph{honest} clients was $0.999998$, so the subtraction was operating
squarely in the catastrophic-cancellation regime. The fix is to compute
the collusion score in \code{f64}.

Half of all per-client weights changed---in the fourth significant digit,

(cross-seed variation halved) rather than \emph{better}; they got
uniformly worse in the mean.

The reason is uncomfortable and worth stating plainly: \textbf{the
floating-point noise was occasionally beneficial by accident.} Before the
fix, two of five seeds happened to land near convergence ($0.3$ and
$0.1$); after it, none do. What looked like partial success was a lottery.
Removing the noise removed the winning tickets along with the losing ones.

\paragraph{Conclusion.} The defect is real and the fix should stay---a
mechanism whose output depends on which rounding errors accumulated is not
one anyone can reason about. But it was never the cause of the
solo-attacker failure. That cause is the one \S\ref{sec:finding3}
describes from the other direction: \textbf{the shared deviation reference
is not robust when the base method is not.} A lone attacker drags that
reference around every round, so every honest client's trace becomes
volatile and every collusion score saturates. The clients are not
misbehaving---the yardstick is. This remains open.

\midrule
\code{dss\_fedavg} (shipped) & $S<0.5 \wedge C>0.8$ & 16.99 $\pm$ 0.017 & 1.175 $\pm$ 0.716 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

Two findings, both uncomfortable for the conjunction's design:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{The conjunction is numerically identical to stability-only,
    to every digit shown.} With two attackers submitting the same crafted
    vector every round, collusion between them is trivially at ceiling
    from round one; it never independently constrains anything.
  \item \textbf{Collusion-only would have caught the one attack the
    conjunction structurally cannot.} $1.08$ vs.\ $16.99$ against
    Persistent Sybil---over $15\times$ better. This quantifies the gate's
    conservatism precisely: refusing to penalize on collusion alone, in
    order to protect a legitimately-noisy honest client, has a real
    measured cost.
\end{itemize}

At this point we could not tell whether the collusion signal was
\emph{redundant} or merely \emph{unmeasurable in this attack model}. The
result described the harness, not the mechanism. Resolving it required a
harder attack.

\subsection{A harder collusion model separates them}
\label{sec:correlated}

Correlated Sybil (\S\ref{sec:attacks}) gives each colluder a shared
objective plus its own fixed offset: correlated, non-identical, and
temporally \emph{stable}---so a stability-only detector must miss it by
construction, and anything that catches it is catching collusion
specifically.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\caption{Experiment 2.9 (1{,}800 rows), $\pm$95\% CI. The

\code{dss\_fedavg} (conjunction)     & 16.99 $\pm$ 0.02 & 17.13 $\pm$ 0.23 & 18.00 $\pm$ 0.18 \\
\code{dssstab\_fedavg} (stability)   & 16.99 $\pm$ 0.02 & 17.13 $\pm$ 0.23 & 18.00 $\pm$ 0.18 \\
\code{dsscoll\_fedavg} (collusion)   & \best{1.08} $\pm$ 0.72 & \best{1.09} $\pm$ 0.73 & \best{1.16} $\pm$ 0.76 \\
\code{krum}                          & 0.297 $\pm$ 0.023 & 0.297 $\pm$ 0.023 & 0.297 $\pm$ 0.023 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\paragraph{The collusion signal is not redundant.} Collusion-only catches
non-identical stable colluders ($1.09$) that stability-only misses
entirely ($17.13$---no better than undefended FedAvg). The two are
${\sim}15\times$ apart, far outside their intervals. The earlier
``numerically identical'' result was an artifact of the attack model. The
signal carries real independent information, and \textbf{the conjunction
is what discards it.}

\paragraph{This does not license flipping the gate to a disjunction.} The
conjunction exists to protect legitimately-noisy honest clients from being
penalized for instability alone---Claim 2's problem, which
\S\ref{sec:fairness} measured as real. Collusion-only scores well here
because every client in this scenario is either honest-and-uncorrelated or
attacking-and-correlated; \S\ref{sec:fairness}'s non-IID minority is the
case that would punish it, and this experiment contains none. What is
established is narrower and sufficient: \textbf{the gate is trading away
something real}, so the combination rule is a genuine open design question
rather than a settled detail.

\paragraph{An unplanned finding: FoolsGold is substantially defeated by
non-identical colluders.} $1.35 \rightarrow 7.54 \rightarrow 9.93$ as
colluders diverge---a $5.6\times$ degradation from the identical case,
well outside the intervals. FoolsGold detects collusion by cosine
similarity between clients' \emph{raw cumulative gradient histories};
giving each attacker a fixed personal offset lowers those pairwise
similarities enough to blunt the pardoning logic, even though the
attackers still share one objective. This is a real limitation of a
published method under a threat model its own paper does not test, found
only because this experiment needed a harder attack for a different reason.

It also sharpens the DSS/FoolsGold contrast. Both are cross-round
collusion detectors, but FoolsGold compares raw gradient histories while
DSS compares scalar deviation traces. The offsets that hide colluders from
the first leave the second intact---collusion-only DSS holds at $1.09$
where FoolsGold degrades to $7.54$. What \S\ref{sec:novelty} positioned as
an architectural distinction is now a measured one.

Krum is unaffected across all three columns ($0.297$), as expected:
selection-based robustness never examines correlation structure at all.

\section{Real-Data Validation}
\label{sec:realdata}

Everything above is measured on synthetic vectors at $d=3$ with no model
and no learning problem. That design isolates the aggregation rule from
every confound, but it means the findings are claims about an aggregation
rule, not yet about federated learning. This section tests whether they
transfer.

\paragraph{Setup.} Real MNIST, a real PyTorch MLP (50{,}890 parameters),
five real clients over real gRPC, six rounds. The attacker is a persistent
Byzantine client with a fixed weight offset of magnitude 20. The
reputation pre-filter is disabled, so what is measured is the
\emph{aggregator's} robustness rather than whether a separate filter
caught the attacker first. \textbf{One seed per cell}---see the scope note
below.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\small
\caption{Experiment 3.1: MNIST held-out accuracy. \emph{Higher is
better}---the opposite direction from every table above. Centralized
baseline: 0.852.}
\label{tab:mnist}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Aggregator} & \textbf{Clean} & \textbf{Poisoned} \\
\midrule
\code{fedavg}       & 0.884 & \bad{0.163} \\
\code{krum}         & 0.857 & \best{0.844} \\
\code{trimmed\_mean} & 0.878 & \best{0.875} \\
\code{centered\_clipping} ($\tau{=}1.0$) & 0.884 & \bad{0.078} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Two of three conclusions transfer cleanly.} On clean data every
method lands within $0.03$ of the others and at or above the centralized
baseline---robustness is close to free when there is nothing to defend
against. Under attack, FedAvg collapses ($0.884 \rightarrow 0.163$) while
Krum and Trimmed Mean hold within a point of their clean accuracy. That is
\S\ref{sec:baseline}'s central result, reproduced on a real model: it is
not an artifact of three-dimensional toy vectors.

\paragraph{The third does not transfer, and that is the finding.}
Centered Clipping at $\tau = 1.0$ scores $0.078$---\textbf{worse than no
defense at all}. Sweeping $\tau$ shows no optimum exists:

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering

toward FedAvg's own number.}
\label{tab:tausweep}

\end{tabular}

Small $\tau$ is too slow to recover; large $\tau$ stops clipping and
\emph{is} FedAvg. Nothing in between reaches Krum's $0.844$.

\paragraph{Why the synthetic experiment could not have shown this.} $\tau$
bounds an $L_2$ norm in \emph{parameter space}, so what it buys per round
depends on how many parameters that norm is spread across.
Table~\ref{tab:tau}'s setting had $d = 3$; this one has $50{,}890$. The
same $\tau$ that moved a 3-dimensional model decisively moves a
50{,}890-dimensional one imperceptibly---and no sweep over $\tau$ at
$d=3$ could reveal a dimensionality dependence, because dimensionality was
not varied. The earlier conclusion that ``$\tau$ must be tuned per
deployment'' was right and understated: \textbf{$\tau$ does not transfer
across model sizes at all}, and a builtin fallback of $1.0$ is a
placeholder, not a default anyone should ship against a real model.

\paragraph{Scope, stated honestly.} One dataset, one architecture, one
attack, one seed per cell, six rounds. The Krum and Trimmed Mean results
are consistent with the synthetic findings and with each other, which is
mild corroboration rather than proof. The Centered Clipping result is
strong in a narrower sense: it is a \emph{negative} result about a
default, and a default that loses to no-defense at every tested $\tau$
does not need many seeds to be worth acting on. What none of this
establishes is behavior over many rounds, under non-IID splits, or against
adaptive attacks.

\section{Comparison Against the Closest Prior Art}
\label{sec:flanders}

FLANDERS~\cite{gabrielli2024} is the closest published method to DSS: a
cross-round pre-aggregation filter composing over an arbitrary base,
explicitly targeting the regime where malicious clients outnumber
legitimate ones. An earlier draft of this paper did not cite it. That
omission is corrected here, and the correction is empirical rather than
textual: we implemented FLANDERS faithfully (MAR(1) fitted by
alternating least squares each round, $\delta(u,v) = \|u-v\|_2^2$,
top-$k$ selection, the paper's cold-start branch) and ran both methods on
identical batches --- 10{,}800 rows, five seeds, twenty rounds, six
attacks, and a malicious-ratio sweep from 20\% to 80\%.

\paragraph{On the adaptive attacker, DSS wins by an order of magnitude.}
Against \texttt{adaptive\_evasion} at 20\% malicious, with undefended
FedAvg at $553.0 \pm 0.07$: DSS reaches $0.64 \pm 0.34$ and FLANDERS
$9.41 \pm 7.46$. The intervals do not overlap.

\paragraph{In FLANDERS's own headline regime, it fails and DSS holds.}
FLANDERS's stated advantage is resilience when malicious clients far
exceed legitimate participants. Against the adaptive attacker at the
ratios its own evaluation uses:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\hline
malicious & FedAvg & DSS & FLANDERS & Krum \\
\hline
20\% & 553.0 & \textbf{0.64} & 9.4 & 0.30 \\
60\% & 1659.1 & \textbf{0.44} & 1901.7 & 2765.0 \\
80\% & 2212.1 & \textbf{7.19} & 2765.0 & 2765.0 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

At 60\% malicious FLANDERS (1901.7) is worse than no defense at all
(1659.1), and at 80\% it has collapsed to the value Krum reaches when
Krum is selecting an attacker. We state this plainly because it cuts
against us as readily as for us: this is the regime FLANDERS claims and
DSS never claimed, and DSS is the method that survives it.

\paragraph{FLANDERS is worse than undefended FedAvg against every Sybil
attack we tested, and the mechanism is structural.} At 20\% malicious,
FLANDERS scores $24.2$ against FedAvg's $17.0$ on
\texttt{persistent\_sybil}, $24.5$ against $17.2$ on
\texttt{correlated\_sybil}, and $25.7$ against $18.0$ on
\texttt{correlated\_sybil\_unstable} --- consistently around 40\% worse.

The cause is not an implementation defect. FLANDERS retains the clients
whose updates best match a forecast of their own past. A colluder that
submits the identical update in every round is therefore the
\emph{easiest} client in the batch to forecast: its anomaly score is near
zero by construction, while honest clients carry training noise and never
forecast perfectly. Top-$k$ selection then keeps the attackers and
discards the honest majority. This threat model does not appear in
FLANDERS's own evaluation, which uses Gaussian, LIE, OPT and AGR-MM ---
all attacks that perturb or optimize, and are therefore unpredictable by
construction.

\paragraph{The two methods fail on opposite shapes.} DSS's conjunction
gate requires \emph{instability} and so misses stable colluders
(\S\ref{sec:ablation}). FLANDERS rewards \emph{forecastability} and so
actively prefers them. Neither dominates the other; they are
complementary blind spots. We think saying so is more useful than either
side claiming priority, and it is the finding this section exists to
report.

\paragraph{A fairness note.} The configuration above pairs FLANDERS with
FedAvg, which is \emph{not} what its authors specify --- they write
$\phi = $ Krum or any other existing robust aggregation heuristic. Paired
as specified, FLANDERS+Krum scores $0.33 \pm 0.15$ on
\texttt{persistent\_sybil} and holds throughout. The precise finding is
therefore that FLANDERS's filter contributes negative value on this
attack family and is carried by whatever robust base it is paired with,
not that the method is broken.

\paragraph{An unplanned finding: the collusion-only ablation dominates.}
Across all six attacks at 20\% malicious, collusion-only DSS
(\S\ref{sec:ablation}) scores $0.310$, $0.255$, $0.251$, $0.265$,
$0.267$ and $0.160$ --- best or tied-best in every column, and one to two
orders of magnitude better than the shipped conjunction gate on five of
the six. It also holds at 60\% malicious. This is the third independent
piece of evidence that the conjunction gate is the limiting component,
after \S\ref{sec:ablation} and \S\ref{sec:correlated}. It does not
license flipping the gate: none of these six attacks includes a
legitimately-noisy honest majority, which is precisely the case the
stability conjunct exists to protect, and Claim 2 would be reopened
without it.

\paragraph{Limits.} All of the above is at $d = 3$. FLANDERS's cost and
signal scale with $d$ in a way DSS's do not, and nothing here measures
that; \S\ref{sec:realdata} is the standing caution that a synthetic
conclusion need not survive a real model. We also forecast every
coordinate rather than subsampling 500 as the paper does, which is exact
at this dimension and is not what a large-model deployment would run.

\section{Conclusions Overturned by Measurement}
\label{sec:overturned}

Four claims in this research line were stated, then disconfirmed by
subsequent measurement. We record them because the discipline that
produced them is the same one that produced the positive results, and
because two were our own diagnoses.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{``The numerical fix will replace the chaotic regime with a
    predictable one.''} It did not. It made results \emph{reproducible}
    (cross-seed CV $1.03 \rightarrow 0.43$) but not better, and the joint
    transient window (\S\ref{sec:joint}) came back \emph{byte-identical}
    after the fix---the same per-seed window lengths and the same
    final-round distances to every printed digit. The two phenomena were
    asserted to share a root cause. They do not.

  \item \textbf{A specific prescribed fix was measurably wrong.} The
    proposed remedy---fall back to an unweighted mean when the weight sum
    drops below $10^{-4}n$---fires on an existing unit test that has clean,
    correct discrimination (its weight sum is $1.38\times10^{-4}$ at
    $n=4$) and replaces the honest consensus with the sybil-dominated
    mean. Prescribing it without testing it would have introduced a defect
    while claiming to remove one.

  \item \textbf{``The conjunction gate is numerically identical to
    stability-only, so the collusion signal may be redundant.''} An
    artifact of the identical-Sybil attack model. Against non-identical
    colluders, collusion-only scores $1.09$ where stability-only scores
    $17.13$ (\S\ref{sec:correlated}). The original result described the
    harness, not the mechanism.

  \item \textbf{``There is a genuine clip-radius optimum at $\tau=4$.''}
    True at $d=3$, and it does not transfer. On a 50{,}890-parameter model
    the curve is monotonic toward FedAvg with no optimum anywhere
    (\S\ref{sec:realdata}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Threats to validity}

\begin{description}
  \item[Synthetic dominance.] Ten of twelve experiments are synthetic at
    $d=3$. \S\ref{sec:realdata} is the only real-model check, and it found
    one conclusion that does not transfer. Others may not either. The
    findings most at risk are the non-IID fairness split
    (\S\ref{sec:fairness}) and the adaptive-attacker results
    (\S\ref{sec:solo}), neither of which has been checked against a real
    model.

  \item[Single-seed real data.] Experiments 3.1/3.2 are one seed per cell.
    Adequate for the negative result about the clipping radius; \emph{not}
    adequate for the comparative Krum / Trimmed Mean claims, which are
    corroboration rather than evidence.

  \item[Attack model coverage.] The adaptive attack is a transparent
    hill-climbing heuristic, deliberately not an optimization-based search
    in the manner of Fang et al.~\cite{fang2020}. A defense evaluated
    against a weaker adaptive attacker than the literature's strongest is
    evaluated optimistically.

  \item[Window length unexplored.] $w=5$ throughout. Whether a shorter
    window shortens \S\ref{sec:joint}'s transient or merely makes it
    noisier is untested.

  \item[FoolsGold degradation is one point, not a curve.] The
    $5.6\times$ degradation (\S\ref{sec:correlated}) was measured at a
    single divergence value. Whether it is gradual or has a threshold is
    unestablished; we report it as an observation, not a characterization.
\end{description}

\subsection{Open design questions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Make the deviation reference robust.} The last unresolved
    cause of a measured DSS failure. Computing the reference with a robust
    statistic---coordinate-wise median or trimmed mean over the batch---
    regardless of which base is wrapped would decouple it from the base's
    fragility, at the cost of one extra reduction per round. The base
    method would then inform only the combine step, which is where
    \S\ref{sec:finding3} already moved it.

  \item \textbf{Decide the combination rule.} \S\ref{sec:correlated}
    establishes the conjunction discards real signal. Flipping to a
    disjunction risks reopening Claim 2. The decisive experiment is an
    ablation against the fairness scenario \emph{with a non-IID minority
    present and no attackers}---which neither existing ablation contains.

  \item \textbf{Characterize the transient.} \S\ref{sec:joint}'s window is
    bounded empirically at 5 seeds in one configuration. Whether it scales
    with attacker fraction, minority size, or window length is unknown.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We argued that Byzantine-robust aggregation rules operating on a single
round's batch share two structural limits independent of their particular
geometric signal, and we measured both against faithful implementations of
twelve published methods across 28{,}331 result rows.

The baseline characterization is, we think, the more durable contribution.
It yields three findings about \emph{published} methods that their own
papers do not report: a survivor-set-size failure mode that splits the
robust family into methods that collapse by ${>}1000\times$ on a
mis-estimated Byzantine fraction and methods that degrade gracefully; a
non-IID fairness cost that appears precisely in methods whose combining
step is a single central-tendency statistic and is near-absent in methods
keeping a wider survivor set; and a $5.6\times$ degradation of FoolsGold
against colluders that share an objective but not a byte stream.

Deviation Stability Scoring, the method we propose, is a narrower result
than we expected when we designed it. It works against the attack shape it
targets and not against the adjacent one. It protects a non-IID minority
asymptotically but suppresses that minority for the first 6--13 rounds.
Its conjunction gate, which exists to prevent exactly that suppression,
demonstrably discards real signal. And until repaired, it made the methods
it wrapped up to $577\times$ worse rather than better---a failure no
amount of design reasoning surfaced, and that appeared immediately on
contact with measurement.

We report it that way because the alternative---reporting the
$161 \rightarrow 1.18$ result and characterizing the rest as future
work---would have been available, and would have been misleading. The
finding we would most want a reader to take from this paper is not DSS.
It is that a wrapper composed over a robust method can silently destroy
the robustness it was meant to augment, that this is invisible to the
design argument, and that a single-seed measurement against a real model
overturned a conclusion ten multi-seed synthetic experiments had left
standing.
